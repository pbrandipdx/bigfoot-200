# Bigfoot 200 — the sub-100 plan

**Race:** Friday August 13, 2027, 12:00 PM · Marble Mountain Sno-Park → Randle, WA
**Course:** 200.1 mi · 44,082 ft gain · 45,563 ft loss · **107-hour limit**
**Goal:** finish under **100:00** — the Western States qualifying standard
**Support:** unsupported. Drop bags at every aid station, no crew, no pacers.
**Today:** September 6, 2026 · 49 weeks out

> The course numbers here come from the 2027 official runner manual. Most of the internet still
> describes a ~208-mile version of this race with aid stations at Windy Ridge and Johnston Ridge.
> That course is retired. Ignore any plan built on it.

---

## Read this first

Three things changed the moment the goal became sub-100 rather than "finish."

**1. Sub-100 is not a fitness target. It is a stopped-time target.**
The gap between 107 and 100 hours is 6.5%. You will not find seven hours by running harder — at
mile 150 there is no harder. You find it by not being stationary. The budget below allows **6 hours
of sleep and 4 hours 12 minutes of aid across 12 stations** — an average stop of 21 minutes,
unsupported, doing your own resupply. A single two-hour sit-down at Chain of Lakes, which is exactly
what happens to people, costs a third of your margin. The discipline that produces sub-100 is
rehearsed in training or it does not exist on race day.

**2. Unsupported is the expensive choice, and it costs roughly what you're trying to save.**
A crew hands you a prepared bottle and a plate and pushes you out. Unsupported you filter, sort a
drop bag, refill, eat, and repack, alone, in the dark, at hour 84. Realistically that is **two to
four hours** across the race versus a competent crew — most of your sub-100 margin. You also lose
pacers, who are permitted from Elk Pass (mile 67.2) onward, through the two sections the race is
known for losing people in, both of which you will now hit alone at night.

That is not a reason to abandon sub-100. It is a reason to be honest that **finding a crew is worth
more than any training block in this plan.** If sub-100 genuinely matters, solving crew is the
highest-leverage thing on this page. If unsupported is fixed, the plan below still works — it just
spends its margin on self-sufficiency instead of speed.

**3. Bighorn would be your first 100-mile finish, eight weeks before a 200.**
Your longest to date is Gorge Waterfalls 100K. The current plan goes 100K → nothing → first 100 at
Bighorn in June → sub-100 at a 200 in August. That is two debuts stacked eight weeks apart, with the
second one unsupported and four nights long. Bighorn stops being a gate and becomes an experiment,
and an experiment is a bad thing to hang an A-race on.

**Recommendation: add a 100-mile finish in winter.** [BURT 100](https://ultraracecalendar.com/calendar/2027/100mi/united-states/)
on Bainbridge Island, WA, Feb 6 2027, sits at the end of Block 2, is close to home, and is PNW
winter — which is the condition you'll be training in anyway. Coldwater Hundred (Goodyear AZ,
Jan 16) is the warm-and-forgiving alternative if you'd rather your first 100 not also be a weather
event. Either one turns Bighorn back into what it's supposed to be: a gate you pass, not a coin flip.

### What the clock puts where

- **The ropes and the knee-deep crossing arrive at dusk.** They sit about 7.4 miles into the
  Blue Lake → Coldwater segment, roughly mile 19.6, around hour 7 — Friday evening, light going.
  Solo, on fixed ropes, into a river. Scout the descriptions and plan lights before you need them.
- **The blast zone is a night crossing.** 25.2 miles, no aid, last non-silty water at segment mile
  6.2, and you're in it from Friday evening to 2:49 AM Saturday. The cutoff pace here is the most
  generous on the course (1.53 mph) because the race knows it's slow. Carry for ten hours.
- **Mt Margaret lands in daylight.** Saturday morning, which is what the manual recommends and the
  best thing about this pacing. Don't trade it away by over-resting on Friday night.
- **Klickitat → Twin Sisters is mostly daylight.** Depart Klickitat 12:15 PM Monday, arrive Twin
  Sisters 11:47 PM. This is the deliberate win in the plan: the notorious 19.4-mile blowdown
  section — faint trail, downed logs, bushwhacking, the section every race report names — gets
  eleven of its twelve hours in the light. **Protect this above everything else.** If you're running
  late by Chain of Lakes, the correct response is to cut sleep, not to arrive at Mission Mountain
  in the dark alone.
- **Blue Lake is the tightest cutoff you'll ever be near.** 42 minutes in hand at mile 12.2, off a
  3,280 ft climb in afternoon heat. It's the only point on the course where the plan is close to a
  cutoff. Don't let a hot first climb become a race-ending one.
- **The last 13 miles are road, 385 ft of gain, and must be run.** 3:23 for 13 miles is 3.8 mph at
  hour 94. This is the single most trainable segment on the course and where most people simply
  stop being able to run. Train for it specifically.

## What changes in training

The volume spine stays. What it's pointed at changes.

### Re-based, because the old baseline was wrong

Your Strava history says you're averaging **8.8 hr/week and 1,700 ft/week** over the last twelve

Block 1 target of 5,000 ft/week was 2.9× your real starting point, not 1.7×. Ramping that fast is

### Five things sub-100 adds that "finish" didn't

**1. Night hours double, and they stop being a checkbox.**
30 cumulative night hours was enough to not be frightened of the dark. You will be moving through
**four nights, alone, unsupported**, roughly 40 hours of it. The target is now **60 cumulative**,
and the shape matters more than the total: at least two efforts that cross a full night and continue
into the next day, and one in Block 4 that crosses **two** nights. Start in October, not November.

**2. Sleep restriction is a recurring session, not a Block 4 stunt.**
Two sessions on under four hours' sleep does not prepare you for 6 hours across 4 days. From Block 2
on, one long day per month starts on deliberately short sleep. In Blocks 4 and 5, practice the
actual protocol: 2 hours down, alarm, up and moving inside 15 minutes. The skill is the waking up,
not the sleeping.

**3. Aid station discipline becomes a measured thing.**
21 minutes average, unsupported, is a trained behavior. On every long day from Block 1: drop a bag
at the car, and time the stop. Target under 10 minutes for a resupply, under 25 with a full change.
Practice with cold hands. Practice at night. The failure mode is not one long stop — it's twelve
average stops that each ran ten minutes over.

**4. Descent volume gets its own prescription.**
45,563 ft of loss, more than the gain. The descent HR gap monitor was already the right instrument;
now it needs volume behind it. From Block 2: **one descent-focused session per week, on rock, late
in the day, on tired legs.** Not loam, not fresh. The plan's original non-negotiable was right and
under-resourced.

**5. Late-race running speed becomes a training target.**
The last 13 miles are road at 3.8 mph and there are runnable stretches all through Lewis River,
Chain of Lakes, and Owens Creek. Everyone can hike at 2.3 mph on day one. Almost nobody can still
*run* on day four, which is exactly where the seven hours live. **Every long day from Block 2 ends
with 30–60 minutes at target race pace on runnable ground.** It will feel pointless and stupid at
the end of an eight-hour day. That is the point.

### Also, because unsupported

- **Carry weight is now a training variable.** The 25.2-mile blast zone crossing with silty water
  means filtering plus a real carry. Do long days with race pack weight from Block 2, not Block 5.
- **Filtering is a rehearsed skill.** Silty water, at night, tired. Practice with the actual filter.
- **Heat acclimation starts in Block 4, not Block 5.** Noon start, blast zone, no shade, reports of
  90°F, and no crew handing you ice. Six sessions was the plan when someone else was carrying your
  cooling. Make it twelve, starting in May.
- **Navigation is on you.** Two sections of this course have "the trail becomes faint and
  disappears" in the *official manual*. Practice night navigation off-trail, solo.

That Bighorn standard is the real gate. Sub-28 with a controlled second half and disciplined aid
says the sub-100 machinery works. Sub-30 with a four-hour death march says you'll finish Bigfoot in
104 hours, which is a fine day out and not what you asked for.

## The gate, restated

The original plan said: finish Bighorn well and Bigfoot is on; DNF or a badly damaged finish and
defer. That still holds, with one addition now that the goal has a number on it.

**Three outcomes at Bighorn, decided in advance:**

- **Under 28 hours, controlled, aid under 90 minutes** → sub-100 is live. Run the plan.
- **28–30 hours, or a finish that cost you two weeks** → Bigfoot is on, sub-100 is not. Race it to
  finish inside 107, enjoy it, qualify another way.
- **DNF, or an injury finish** → defer Bigfoot. There is another one next year and there is only
  one of you.

Write that down now, in September, while it's abstract and costs nothing. In June it will cost
something.

## What still needs your Garmin

Everything above is trackable from Strava. These are not, and the tracker leaves them blank rather
than guessing: descent HR gap, aerobic decoupling, HRV, resting HR, sleep and Body Battery, and the
three the original plan already flagged as missing — **hill score, endurance score, running
tolerance.** Running tolerance matters more now than it did: the vert ramp in Block 1 is the
steepest relative jump in the whole plan, and that number is the guardrail on it.
