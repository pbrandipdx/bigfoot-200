Six hours across four nights averages an hour and a half a night — against your normal seven-plus, that's roughly 24 hours of sleep debt by the finish, accumulated while moving. The race allows up to six hours at any one sleep station; you're using two and a half at most. The constraint is the clock, not the rules.

The sleep is your second reserve. Three hours of schedule slack absorbs the ordinary — a bad stomach hour, a wrong turn, weather. Twin Sisters is the deliberate one: cut it to 45 minutes and you claw back 1:45. Together that's about five and a half hours against the 100:00 goal, spent in that order, and you never touch the second one before mile 171.

Why the big nap is last. Twin Sisters sits at mile 171 with 29 miles left, after both of the sections this race is known for, and the last 13 of those miles are road that has to be run at 3.8 mph. Most people arrive at mile 171 having spent their sleep early, then walk a finish they could have run.

Where the vertical comes from

You need to go from 1,700 ft a week to 4,500 by mid-December, then 6,000 → 7,500 → 9,500 → 12,000 by next summer. Lake Oswego itself is vertically poor — Tryon Creek, Iron Mountain and Mount Sylvania all sit around 110–170 ft per mile, so you'd need ten miles to bank fifteen hundred feet. The usable vert starts 15 minutes north.

The seasons work in your favor. Your winter volume — December through April, 6,000 to 7,500 ft a week — has to come from Forest Park, the low Gorge and the Coast Range, all of which stay open. The 9,500–12,000 ft weeks land in late spring and summer, which is exactly when Mount Defiance, Table Mountain, the Loowit and Monitor Ridge come back. Nothing in the plan asks for a venue that's closed when you need it.

Course-specific — Mount St Helens

SR 504 is closed by a landslide and isn't expected to reopen until 2027. That removes the entire northwest approach — Johnston Ridge, Coldwater Peak, and vehicle access to the Coldwater Lake area. Plan every recon trip from the south on FR 83/81 out of Cougar, or the northeast on FR 25/26/99 out of Randle.
  • Worm Flows from Marble Mountain Sno-Park — 12 mi, 5,700 ftThis is literally the Bigfoot start line. Winter route: climbing permits are free and self-issued Dec 1 – Mar 31, plus a Washington Sno-Park permit. Requires ice axe, crampons and avalanche awareness — it's a mountaineering day, not a training run. ~2h15 drive.
  • Monitor Ridge from Climbers Bivouac — 10 mi, 4,500 ftThe summer summit route, late June through October. 2,500 vertical feet of boulder fields under ash pumice that shreds skin in a fall, then 1,000 ft of scree — two steps forward, one back. Gloves, gaiters, poles. This is the exact surface Bigfoot punishes you on.
  • Loowit Trail #216 — 32 mi, 6,000 ftThe full circumnavigation and the best single course simulator available: pumice fields gouged with deep gullies, lava beds, boulder fields, ash slopes, three major washouts with loose canyon walls. Sparse water, no shade. A 6,000 ft day and course reconnaissance in one outing — the highest-value weekend of the whole build.
  • Ape Canyon — 11 mi, 1,400–2,500 ftThe runnable ash trail. Steady grade on cinders and pumice, ends at the Loowit so you can extend it. Sources disagree on the gain by over 1,000 ft — measure it yourself the first time.
  • Norway Pass — 4.2 mi, 855 ftActually on the course. Low gain and a three-hour drive via Randle, so go for course knowledge, not vertical.

Rocky descent — ranked

Descent durability on rock is a named priority: the race has 45,563 ft of loss, more than its gain. These are ranked by how much rock is actually under you on the way down.

  1. Elk–Kings loop — rock steps, a fixed rope, precipitous ridge. The best trainer, period.
  2. Table Mountain / Heartbreak Ridge — 700 ft in half a mile on loose rock and marked talus.
  3. Mount Defiance–Starvation Ridge — multiple cairned talus fields and a narrow ridge crest.
  4. Monitor Ridge — ash-covered boulders then scree. Course-identical surface.
  5. Loowit Trail — boulder fields, pumice gullies, washouts with loose rock.
  6. Munra Point — chutes, fixed ropes, real exposure. A skills day, not a volume day. People have been seriously injured falling from the summit ridges.
  7. Nesmith Point — scree slopes and basalt faces up high.
  8. Mount Talbert — only 685 ft, but genuinely rocky, and fifteen minutes away. Use it as a weekday footing drill.

Three things that changed when the goal got a number

1 · Sub-100 is a stopped-time target, not a fitness one

The gap between 107 and 100 hours is 6.5%. You won't find seven hours by running harder — at mile 150 there is no harder. You find it by not being stationary. The budget allows six hours of sleep and 4:12 of aid across twelve stations, unsupported, doing your own resupply. A single two-hour sit-down at Chain of Lakes — which is exactly what happened to the runner who finished second in 2022 — costs a third of your margin.

That discipline is rehearsed in training or it doesn't exist on race day.

2 · Unsupported costs roughly what you're trying to save

A crew hands you a prepared bottle and pushes you out. Unsupported, you filter, sort a drop bag, refill, eat and repack, alone, in the dark, at hour 84. Realistically that's two to four hours across the race — most of your margin. You also lose pacers, permitted from Coldwater Lake at mile 37.4, through the two sections this race is known for losing people in.

Finding a crew is worth more than any training block on this page. That's not a reason to abandon sub-100 — it's a reason to be honest about where the hours are. If unsupported is fixed, the plan still works; it just spends its margin on self-sufficiency instead of speed.

3 · Bighorn would be your first 100, eight weeks before a 200

Your longest to date is Gorge Waterfalls 100K. The original plan goes 100K → nothing → first 100 at Bighorn in June → sub-100 at a 200 in August. Two debuts stacked eight weeks apart, the second one unsupported and four nights long. Bighorn stops being a gate and becomes an experiment, and an experiment is a bad thing to hang an A-race on.

The fix, now pencilled in: BURT 100, Bainbridge Island, Saturday Feb 6 2027. It sits in week 21 of Block 2, close to home, in PNW winter — the condition you'll be training in anyway — and it leaves the following week as recovery before Block 3 opens.

It's a 54K loop run three times, which sounds monotonous and is exactly right for what you need out of it. A loop course means you pass a drop bag roughly every five hours, so it's the only race on the calendar where you can rehearse the aid-station discipline sub-100 actually depends on — twelve timed transitions, unsupported, one of them at 3 AM.

BURT is not a Western States qualifier. It earns nothing but the thing you actually need — a 100-mile finish and a full night alone. Your qualifiers are Gorge Waterfalls 100K (17-hour cutoff) and Bighorn 100 (35-hour cutoff), both already in the plan. Worth noting the old Gorge target of "16–18 hours" straddled that 17-hour cutoff; the sub-100 target of 14–15 clears it with room.

Five things sub-100 adds

  • Night hours double, and stop being a checkboxThirty cumulative hours was enough to not be frightened of the dark. You'll move through four nights alone, roughly 40 hours of it. Target is 60 cumulative — and the shape matters more than the total: two efforts crossing a full night into the next day, and one in Block 4 crossing two. Start in October, not November.
  • Sleep restriction becomes a recurring sessionTwo sessions on under four hours doesn't prepare you for six hours across four days. From Block 2, one long day a month starts on deliberately short sleep. In Blocks 4 and 5, rehearse the protocol: two hours down, alarm, up and moving inside fifteen minutes. The skill is the waking up.
  • Aid discipline becomes a measured thing21 minutes average, unsupported, is trained behavior. On every long day: drop a bag at the car and time the stop. Under 10 minutes for a resupply, under 25 with a full change. Practice with cold hands, and at night. The failure mode isn't one long stop — it's twelve average stops that each ran ten minutes over.
  • Descent gets its own prescription45,563 ft of loss, more than the gain. One descent-focused session a week from Block 2 — on rock, late in the day, on tired legs. Not loam, not fresh.
  • Late-race running speed becomes a targetEveryone can hike 2.3 mph on day one. Almost nobody can still run on day four, which is exactly where the seven hours live. Every long day from Block 2 ends with 30–60 minutes at race pace on runnable ground. It will feel pointless at the end of an eight-hour day. That's the point.

Because unsupported

  • Carry weight is a training variable. The 25.2-mile blast zone crossing with silty water means filtering plus a real carry. Long days with race pack weight from Block 2, not Block 5.
  • Filtering is a rehearsed skill. Silty water, at night, tired, with the actual filter.
  • Heat acclimation starts in Block 4. Noon start, blast zone, no shade, reports of 90°F, and nobody handing you ice. Six sessions was the plan when someone else carried your cooling. Make it twelve, starting in May.
  • Navigation is on you. Two sections have "the trail becomes faint and disappears" in the official manual. Practice night navigation off-trail, solo.
  • Gear checks are random and mid-race. You carry the full mandatory list the whole way with no crew to hold anything. Build the actual race pack early.

Not coming from Strava

Strava carries time, distance, elevation and activity timing, so everything in Training updates on its own. These don't exist in Strava at all — they live in Garmin, and they stay blank here rather than guessed.

  • Hill score, endurance score, running toleranceGarmin-computed, and the three the plan flags as missing. Running tolerance is the guardrail on Block 1's vertical ramp.
  • Descent HR gapHeart rate split by climb versus descent inside a single activity. The best durability predictor available — baseline 15–20 bpm, and the fatigued pattern is ~2.
  • Aerobic decouplingPace and HR streams, first half versus second half. Target under 5%.
  • HRV, resting HR, sleep, Body BatteryOvernight physiology. Garmin only.
  • Night hoursDerivable from activity timing against local sunset and sunrise — wired up, currently zero.

Monitors — thresholds, not goals

Descent HR gap

Average HR on sustained descent versus sustained climb, on any long day. Baseline 15–20 bpm; the fatigued pattern is about 2. Under 10 bpm on a day that should feel comfortable means under-recovery. Two in a row, cut the week.

Aerobic decoupling and zone discipline

Pace-to-HR, first half versus second half of long days. Target under 5%; above 8% consistently means the day was too hard or you're under-recovered. Long days sit 125–140 bpm — more than 15% of a long day above 150 and it was a workout, not a long day.

Heart rate zones — use the Garmin ones

Measured max 205. Z1 under 121 · Z2 121–140 · Z3 141–163 · Z4 164–184 · Z5 185+. Strava's age-formula zones run about 20 beats low and should be ignored.

VO2 max — monitor, do not chase

42.0 at baseline; expect 43–46 by Bighorn as a byproduct, and expect it to decline in Blocks 4 and 5 under peak volume. That's normal. For a hundred-hour race it's close to irrelevant — if you find yourself training to move that number, you're training for the wrong race.

HRV, resting HR, sleep, Body Battery

HRV — trend only, 30-day rolling baseline. Single low day: ignore. Seven-day average down more than 10%: cut volume 30% that week. "Unbalanced" for seven days: three full days off.

Resting HR — up 5 bpm sustained over five days: back off. Up 8: stop and check for illness.

Sleep — train rested, race deprived. Seven-plus hours a night through Blocks 1–4; sleep score 75+ the two nights before any long day.

Body Battery — 70+ on the morning of a long day. Under 50, downgrade the session. Overnight recharge under 30 for three straight nights means accumulated fatigue.

Non-negotiables

  • Night hours accumulate on schedule. Zero so far, and it's the cheapest gap to close.
  • Bighorn is a gate, not a formality. Decide the rule now.
  • Descent training happens on rock, not loam, and late in the day.
  • Feet get managed from hour five, not hour nine. Practice in training.
  • Eat every 40 minutes on every long day, including when you don't want to.

Course facts, and how these numbers are built

200.1 miles · 44,082 ft gain · 45,563 ft loss · 107-hour limit · noon Friday start. Twelve full aid stations, drop bags at every one, five sleep stations, six-hour maximum at any one of them. Net 1,481 ft downhill — you start high on Mount St Helens and finish in the Cispus valley.

Every figure comes from the official Destination Trail Runner Manual. Segment miles sum to exactly 200.1, gains to 44,082, losses to 45,563, hour allowances to 107.0. Most of the internet still describes a ~208-mile version with aid stations at Windy Ridge and Johnston Ridge — that course is retired. CalTopo measures the course short at 184.5 miles; the manual's mileages are the accurate ones.

Two errors in the manual, corrected here

The Quartz Ridge → Chain of Lakes row prints 2.6 mph where 16.2 mi ÷ 7.5 h = 2.16. And the prose for Start → Blue Lake says 2,980/2,490 ft where the chart says 3,280/2,743 — the chart figure is the one that sums correctly to the totals.

How the training numbers are computed
  • Time on feet — moving time from Walk, Hike, Run and Stair-Stepper. Weights, swims and ball sports excluded.
  • Vertical — Strava elevation gain in feet. Treadmill and stair-stepper report zero gain, so real climbing is understated whenever the vert came from a machine.
  • Miles — Walk, Hike and Run distance only.
  • Longest effort — the single longest activity in the week, not the daily total.
  • Weeks run Monday to Sunday.
